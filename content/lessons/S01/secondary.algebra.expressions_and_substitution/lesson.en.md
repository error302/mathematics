# Algebraic Expressions, Substitution, and Equivalence

## 1. Variables as Grouped Placeholders

In algebra, a variable such as $x$ is a placeholder for an entire number—including its sign. Whenever you substitute a negative value $x = -a$ into an expression, always enclose it in parentheses:

$$x^2 \Big|_{x = -3} = (-3)^2 = (-3) \times (-3) = +9$$

Without parentheses, $-3^2$ means $-(3^2) = -9$, which answers a different question.

## 2. Two-Variable Substitution and Double Negatives

When evaluating an expression such as $p(x - y) + qxy$ for $x > 0$ and $y < 0$:
1. Substitute both variables in parentheses: $x - y = x - (-|y|) = x + |y|$.
2. Multiply the product term carefully: $q(x)(y) < 0$.

## 3. Distributive Simplification and Like Terms

The distributive property over addition and subtraction states that for all $a, b, c$:

$$a(bx + c) - d(ex - f) = abx + ac - dex + df = (ab - de)x + (ac + df)$$

Notice the sign of the final constant term $+df$: subtracting $d$ groups of $-f$ adds $+df$.

Two terms are **like terms** only when they share the exact same variable factors raised to the exact same exponents (for example, $7x$ and $-2x$ combine to $5x$, whereas $7x^2$ and $-2x$ are unlike terms and remain separate).
